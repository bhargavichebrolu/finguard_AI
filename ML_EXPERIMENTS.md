## Experimental design

The selection rule and threshold objective were fixed before any candidate was scored: see [ADR 0002](docs/decisions/0002-phase-2-decisions.md).

| Element | Decision |
|---|---|
| Features | Seven features in schema `1.0.0`, derived only from the four prediction inputs |
| Excluded | Balances, identifiers, `isFraud`, `isFlaggedFraud`, raw `step` |
| Fitting | Training partition: steps 1 to 322 (3,633 fraud rows, 0.0819%) |
| Tuning, selection, threshold | Validation partition: steps 323 to 377 (570 fraud rows, 0.0586%) |
| Final evaluation, once, after the freeze | Test partition: steps 378 to 743 (4,010 fraud rows, 0.4196%) |
| Models | Logistic Regression, Decision Tree, Random Forest, XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost |
| Imbalance strategies | Original distribution, class weighting, SMOTE, ADASYN |

## What the data implies

### 1. Expect far lower scores than published PaySim results
Many public PaySim notebooks report near-perfect scores using balance columns, which this project excludes. A modest honest score should be expected and is not a failure, and any near-perfect score should be treated as a leakage alarm.

### 2. Base rates differ strongly across partitions
- **PR-AUC and precision cannot be compared directly across partitions**, because both depend on prevalence. Report each alongside its baseline, which for PR-AUC is the partition's fraud rate.
- **Precision is likely to look better on test simply because fraud is more common there.** This should be reported as prior shift, not as model skill.
- **ROC-AUC is less affected by prevalence**, though it can look flattering at this imbalance, so it does not replace PR-AUC.

### 3. Most of the data is trivially legitimate
All training fraud is in two types. Models will learn this quickly, so overall scores partly reflect that easy split. The transaction-type baseline shows whether a model does more than recognise the type.

### 4. Rule baseline
`isFlaggedFraud` flags 3, 0 and 13 rows across train, validation and test, and every flagged row is fraud. It serves as the existing-rule baseline: perfect precision and almost no recall.

### 5. Compute constraints
The development machine has 16 logical processors and 15.7 GB of RAM, with under 2 GB free at the start of Phase 2. A timing probe on 10% of training rows showed all six families are feasible, so none was dropped.

## Validation findings

Every figure in this section is on validation unless it says otherwise. Charts are in [experiments/figures/models/](experiments/figures/models/). Every run, including tuning, is in `experiments/results/phase2/all_runs.csv`.

### Selected model

**XGBoost on the original class distribution, with a threshold of 0.02842.** It was chosen by the [pre-registered rule](docs/decisions/0002-phase-2-decisions.md), applied in this order:

| Step | Result |
|---|---|
| Gates | All six families passed: each beat the type-rate baseline on validation and in every rolling fold, with seed variation under 10% and no failed run |
| Top by mean PR-AUC across seeds | CatBoost, 0.3522 |
| Statistically indistinguishable from the top | XGBoost (0.3474) and LightGBM (0.3457). Random Forest, Decision Tree and Logistic Regression were separated from it |
| Highest recall at FPR of at most 0.1%, within 1 point | XGBoost and LightGBM, both 41.23%. CatBoost's 40.18% fell below the 40.23% cut-off |
| Lowest median single-row prediction time | XGBoost 2.06 ms, LightGBM 2.13 ms |

**The top three are effectively tied, and this result does not show that XGBoost is better than them.** CatBoost missed the recall tie by less than one fraud row (229 caught against a limit of 229.3), and the final tie-break was a 0.07 ms difference. The rule was needed because the data could not separate them. Outside the rule, XGBoost was also more consistent than LightGBM in the last rolling fold (0.3805 against 0.2378) and trained faster than CatBoost (18 s against 61 s).

### Threshold selection

The threshold is the lowest one with a false positive rate of at most 0.1% on validation. A false positive rate was chosen because it does not depend on prevalence, whereas precision and F1 do, and prevalence differs sevenfold between validation and test. The result is **precision 21.2%, recall 41.2%, F1 0.280, FPR 0.090%** (235 frauds caught, 335 missed, 873 false alerts).

- **It stops short of the cap because of tied scores.** The model produces only 3,711 distinct scores. A block of 264 rows sits just below the threshold, and including it would mean 1,135 false positives against 972 allowed.
- **The cap is not a guarantee on new steps.** Resampling steps gives an out-of-bag FPR averaging 0.118%, with a 95% interval of 0.008% to 0.327%, so it can exceed the cap on steps not used to set it.
- **The PRD's candidates sit far from the cap.** Thresholds of 0.30 to 0.70 give precision of 88% to 99%, but recall of only 24% to 13%, which is why the threshold looks low on a 0 to 1 scale.
- **The last few frauds are expensive.** Capping FPR at 0.05% instead catches 223 frauds with 310 false alerts, so the frozen threshold pays 563 more false alerts for 12 more frauds. The threshold with the best F1 (0.0763) catches 194 frauds with 91 false alerts, and F1 0.454. The objective was fixed in advance, so it stands, but the choice should be revisited if the Phase 3 alert review has limited capacity.
- **Scores are not calibrated probabilities.** Rows scoring 0.1 to 0.2 were fraud 31% of the time. Treat a score as a ranking, not as the chance of fraud.

### Temporal prevalence shift

The fraud rate rises from 0.082% in train to 0.420% in test because legitimate volume collapses, not because fraud increases.

- Fraud per step is stable (11.3, 10.4 and 11.0 across train, validation and test), while legitimate rows per step are 13,758 in train, 17,684 in validation and only 2,600 in test.
- Of the 0.338 point rise from train to test, 0.317 comes from the mix of sparse and normal steps, and 0.021 from rates within them.
- At training's legitimate volume, test's fraud rate would be 0.080%, almost the same as train.
- Amount and type barely drift from train to validation (PSI of 0.03 or less). Hour shows moderate drift (PSI of 0.09 to 0.15).

**Test precision and PR-AUC will therefore rise regardless of skill**, and must not be read as improvement.

### Sparse and normal steps

A step is **sparse if it has fewer than 1,000 rows**. The definition counts rows only, uses no labels, and is never a model feature. Results at cut-offs of 500 and 2,000 are saved in `temporal_drift.json`.

| XGBoost at the frozen threshold | Sparse steps | Normal steps |
|---|---:|---:|
| Rows | 1,479 | 971,694 |
| Fraud | 160 | 410 |
| Fraud rate | 10.82% | 0.042% |
| PR-AUC | 0.8191 | 0.1700 |
| PR-AUC divided by the fraud rate | 7.6 | 403 |
| ROC-AUC | 0.9718 | 0.9255 |
| Recall | 95.6% | 20.0% |
| False positive rate | 13.95% | 0.071% |

These are two different problems, and the overall recall of 41% describes neither. Sparse steps are 0.15% of rows but supply 153 of the 235 frauds caught. In normal steps, which are what the platform would mostly see, the model finds one fraud in five. The very different PR-AUC values partly reflect the fraud rate, so the two regimes cannot be ranked by PR-AUC alone.

### Reliance on hour, and what SHAP shows

SHAP values were calculated with TreeSHAP on 20,570 validation rows: all 570 frauds, and 20,000 reweighted legitimate rows. They add up to the model's output, with a largest error of 5.4e-07.

- **On fraud rows, hour, amount and type matter about equally** (1.48, 1.46 and 1.36 log-odds, summed within each group). Across all rows, type and destination dominate (2.30), because the model mostly learns that payments and cash-ins are not fraud here.
- **Hour acts largely as a stand-in for sparse steps.** Its contribution averages 2.69 in sparse steps and 0.35 in normal ones, and it is largest at hours 2 to 7, when training and validation steps are almost always sparse.
- **The model relies on this.** Removing hour features from CatBoost, the top family by PR-AUC, cut PR-AUC from 0.3527 to 0.1592 and recall from 40.2% to 19.0%. Removing amount features cut PR-AUC to 0.2327. These two runs used CatBoost, not the selected XGBoost.
- **This reliance is the main risk on test.** In validation, sparse steps never occur between hours 9 and 23, but in test 38% to 73% of the steps at those hours are sparse. The model's learned link between hour and sparse steps may not hold there.

SHAP explains this model's output. It does not establish that any feature causes fraud, and these patterns may reflect how the simulator was programmed.

### Imbalance strategies

No strategy was best everywhere, and oversampling did not generally help.

- The original distribution was best for Logistic Regression, Random Forest and XGBoost, and class weighting was best for CatBoost (0.3481 against 0.3425).
- SMOTE clearly helped only the Decision Tree (0.2874 against 0.2500). ADASYN scored below SMOTE in every family except Logistic Regression.
- Changing the oversampling ratio to 0.02 or 0.3 changed LightGBM's PR-AUC by at most 0.024.

### LightGBM's collapse was numerical

**The LightGBM row of the matrix is not evidence about imbalance strategies.** It scored 0.0618 on the original distribution and 0.0011 with class weighting, while the other tree models held. A diagnostic found the cause (see the table in [Results](#results)):

- With class weighting, split gains explode past 1e15. Training stops early, leaving as few as two distinct scores.
- Turning off LightGBM's `boost_from_average` stops this. With it off, all four fits score between 0.3052 and 0.3480, at both baseline and tuned settings.
- That setting is what was shown to matter. The exact mechanism inside LightGBM was not traced.

So SMOTE appeared to rescue LightGBM mainly by avoiding this divergence. The diagnostic ran after selection, so **those fits were not candidates and the selection stands**. Its best fit (0.3480, one seed, not bootstrapped) is close to the top three, so it gives no reason to think the choice was wrong, but a configuration that was never searched cannot be ruled out. Any future LightGBM candidate should turn this setting off.

### Other limits

- **Transfers are harder than cash-outs:** recall 31.9% against 50.5%, and false positive rate 0.81% against 0.06%.
- **Amounts at the simulator's cap:** 797 rows and only 10 frauds, of which 7 were caught with no false alerts. This sample is too small to rely on.
- **Search coverage:** tuning only kept each family's best matrix strategy, so other pairings were not searched. Logistic Regression shows 3 tuning runs rather than 4, because one sample matched its baseline.
- **CatBoost's single-row time** is not strictly single-threaded, because its thread count cannot be changed after fitting.

If the model behaves within each regime as it did on validation, then applying validation's recall and false positive rate per regime to test's row counts (published in Phase 1) gives:

| Regime | Test fraud | Test legitimate | Recall | FPR | Frauds caught | False alerts |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Sparse | 2,812 | 22,934 | 95.6% | 13.95% | 2,689 | 3,199 |
| Normal | 1,198 | 928,800 | 20.0% | 0.071% | 240 | 659 |
| **Total** | **4,010** | **951,734** | **73.0%** | **0.405%** | **2,929** | **3,858** |

That predicts **recall near 73%, precision near 43%, and a false positive rate about four times the 0.1% cap**, purely because sparse steps make up far more of test. A large miss in either direction would mean behaviour within the regimes changed, which the hour finding above makes plausible.
